
Statute of Limitations Dynamics and Post-Judgment Asset Rules in Pennsylvania

The temporal window for the legal recovery of medical debts in the Commonwealth is governed primarily by the statute of limitations for written contracts. Pursuant to 42 Pa. C.S. § 5525, the statutory period for commencing an action on a written contract is four years. Because most medical billing is predicated on a signed "Assignment of Benefits" or "Financial Responsibility" agreement executed at the point of intake, this four-year window typically dictates the timeframe in which a provider may initiate a civil lawsuit to secure a judgment. It is critical to note that while the No Surprises Act (NSA) prohibits the initial balance bill, the statute of limitations continues to run on the underlying debt unless tolled; however, any attempt to sue for a balance that is federally prohibited under the NSA would likely be dismissed as a matter of law.

Once a medical provider successfully obtains a civil judgment, the transition from a debt to an enforceable judgment introduces a new set of procedural rules regarding asset seizure and execution. In Pennsylvania, a judgment creditor may seek to satisfy the debt through a writ of execution, allowing for the attachment of the debtor's property or the garnishment of wages. However, Pennsylvania law provides significant statutory exemptions designed to prevent the total indigence of the debtor.

Under the Pennsylvania Rules of Civil Procedure and relevant state statutes, certain assets are exempt from execution. These protections include:

Homestead Exemptions: Protections for a primary residence, which limit the ability of medical creditors to force the sale of a home to satisfy a judgment.
Wage Garnishment Caps: Restrictions on the percentage of disposable earnings that can be garnished, ensuring the debtor retains sufficient funds for basic subsistence.
Personal Property Exemptions: Protections for essential household goods, clothing, and tools of a trade, which are generally shielded from seizure by a sheriff’s sale.
Furthermore, the interaction between the FCEUA and post-judgment collections remains active. Even after a judgment is entered, a creditor’s attempts to collect must not violate the conduct rules established under 73 P.S. § 2270.4. If a creditor utilizes deceptive practices to induce a voluntary payment on a judgment that they know to be based on an unlawful NSA balance bill, the consumer may maintain a cause of action for damages under the UTPCPL, potentially offsetting the judgment amount with statutory treble damages.
