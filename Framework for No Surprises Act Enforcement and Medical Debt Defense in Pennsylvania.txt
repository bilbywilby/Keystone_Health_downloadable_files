Legal and Administrative Framework for No Surprises Act Enforcement and Medical Debt Defense in Pennsylvania
Federal Statutory Architecture and Consumer Protections
The enactment of Title I of Division BB of the Consolidated Appropriations Act, 2021, designated as the No Surprises Act (NSA), established a nationwide statutory shield intended to eliminate balance billing in circumstances where healthcare consumers lack direct control over their choice of medical service providers. Codified across Sections 2799A-1 through 2799A-3 of the Public Health Service Act (PHSA) and implemented primarily under 45 C.F.R. Part 149, the federal statute prohibits out-of-network healthcare providers and clinical facilities from billing covered individuals for amounts exceeding standard in-network cost-sharing obligations.
The statutory protections are operationalized across three core clinical domains:
All emergency care services delivered at out-of-network emergency departments, hospital outpatient facilities, and standalone urgent care centers, extending through patient stabilization and including post-stabilization services unless rigorous statutory written-consent requirements are executed.
Non-emergency surgical, diagnostic, or ancillary care rendered by out-of-network healthcare professionals—specifically including anesthesiologists, radiologists, pathologists, hospitalists, and assistant surgeons—operating within an in-network hospital or ambulatory surgical center, where the patient did not knowingly execute a valid statutory waiver under federal timing standards.
Out-of-network air ambulance services, governed under 45 C.F.R. § 149.230, which explicitly bans balance billing for emergency and non-emergency air transportation while leaving ground ambulance regulation to state statutory frameworks.
Under this federal regime, a patient's total financial obligation is legally capped at their applicable in-network cost-sharing amount, encompassing copayments, coinsurance, and annual deductible requirements set by their specific health benefit plan. This cost-sharing parameter must be calculated based on the Qualifying Payment Amount (QPA), defined generally as the insurer's median contracted rate for the specific service within the relevant geographic region.
When an out-of-network billing dispute arises between a provider and an insurer, federal law mandates that the patient be removed from the payment dispute. The health plan and out-of-network provider must enter a mandatory thirty-business-day Open Negotiation Period pursuant to 45 C.F.R. § 149.500. If negotiations fail to achieve a settlement, either party may initiate the Federal Independent Dispute Resolution (IDR) process within four business days following the conclusion of open negotiations.
This arrangement ensures that billing disagreements are adjudicated exclusively between institutional entities using certified IDR entities, preventing healthcare providers from utilizing patient collection mechanisms as financial leverage.
Pennsylvania Statutory Overlay and Unfair Debt Collection Protections
While federal provisions establish substantive rights against balance billing, administrative enforcement operates through a dual-regulatory scheme involving federal agencies and state insurance regulators. In Pennsylvania, federal surprise billing prohibitions operate alongside Commonwealth laws regulating insurance practices, trade deception, and consumer debt collection.
The primary state regulatory body overseeing health coverage plans in the Commonwealth is the Pennsylvania Insurance Department (PID), which enforces insurer compliance through its Consumer Services Bureau. The PID maintains a toll-free consumer hotline (1-877-881-6388) and online intake portal to process formal coverage complaints.
Concurrently, the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) oversees federal enforcement via the No Surprises Help Desk (1-800-985-3059). This dual structure allows consumers to lodge administrative complaints simultaneously with state and federal oversight bodies, creating regulatory pressure against non-compliant providers and carriers.

The enforcement of medical bill disputes in Pennsylvania is further defined by statutory restrictions governing debt collection tactics. The Fair Credit Extension Uniformity Act (FCEUA), codified at 73 P.S. § 2270.1 et seq., serves as the principal state statute regulating debt collection practices within Pennsylvania.
A critical legal distinction exists between the federal Fair Debt Collection Practices Act (FDCPA, 15 U.S.C. § 1692 et seq.) and the Pennsylvania FCEUA. The federal FDCPA applies primarily to third-party collection agencies and attorney collectors, whereas the Pennsylvania FCEUA explicitly extends strict debt collection conduct rules directly to original creditors, including hospital systems, physician practices, and ambulatory facilities under 73 P.S. § 2270.4(b).
The statutory interaction between the FCEUA and Pennsylvania’s Unfair Trade Practices and Consumer Protection Law (UTPCPL), codified at 73 P.S. § 201-1 et seq., provides powerful legal remedies for consumers. Pursuant to 73 P.S. § 2270.5(a), any violation of the FCEUA automatically constitutes a per se violation of the UTPCPL. Under 73 P.S. § 201-9.2, an established UTPCPL violation creates a private right of action permitting courts to award actual damages, statutory treble damages, and reasonable attorney fees.
Consequently, if a medical provider or third-party collection agency attempts to collect an unlawful balance bill while aware of an active No Surprises Act dispute, or fails to report the account as disputed to credit reporting agencies under 73 P.S. § 2270.4 and 15 U.S.C. § 1692e(8), the entity faces statutory exposure under both state and federal law.
Statute of Limitations Dynamics and Post-Judgment Asset Rules in Pennsylvania
Managing disputed medical claims requires an accurate understanding of Pennsylvania's procedural statutes of limitations and post-judgment enforcement rules. Under 42 Pa.C.S. § 5525(a), Pennsylvania establishes a strict four-year statute of limitations for actions founded upon express or implied contracts, open-end accounts, and promissory notes. Because medical billing obligations are legally classified as contract debts, healthcare providers and assigned debt collection entities have a four-year window from the date of initial contractual default to file a judicial collection action.
The statute of limitations clock begins to run on the date of breach or default, which generally occurs thirty days after the initial missed payment due date. However, a major legal vulnerability involves actions that restart this four-year statutory clock. Under Pennsylvania common law and judicial applications of 42 Pa.C.S. § 5525, making a partial payment toward an outstanding medical balance—or providing a written acknowledgment admitting the validity of the underlying obligation—resets the four-year limitations period from the date of that payment or writing.
In the context of an ongoing No Surprises Act dispute, issuing a partial payment intended as a good-faith gesture carries severe legal consequences:
It resets the creditor's four-year judicial enforcement clock under 42 Pa.C.S. § 5525, granting the billing entity a fresh four-year window to initiate legal proceedings.
It generates an evidentiary admission regarding the validity and enforceability of the billed amount, which healthcare entities may attempt to construe as a waiver of federal balance billing protections or an accord and satisfaction.
If a medical debt claim proceeds to legal judgment, Pennsylvania statutory law establishes specific asset protections that distinguish the Commonwealth from many other state jurisdictions. Under 42 Pa.C.S. § 8127, wages, salaries, and commissions held by an employer are shielded from attachment and garnishment for standard civil judgment debts, including medical bills and personal consumer obligations.
While wage garnishment is prohibited for standard medical judgments, judgment creditors retain the legal authority to execute bank account levies. Under 42 Pa.C.S. § 8123, Pennsylvania provides a general statutory property exemption of only $300 for judgment debtors, leaving liquid bank deposits above this baseline vulnerable to attachment and sheriff levy.

Analysis of Provider, Insurer, and Collection Dispute Notices
Standardized dispute templates translate federal and state legal provisions into operational demands:
Provider Dispute Notice (Letter 1): Cites federal protections under 45 C.F.R. §§ 149.210, 149.220, or 149.230, requiring the healthcare facility or provider to elect the specific clinical basis for the billing charge. The letter confirms that the patient did not execute a valid written consent waiver, explicitly limits dispute scope to out-of-network balance charges, demands an immediate hold on collection activities, and provides formal notice of regulatory escalation to the PID and CMS if unresolved within thirty days.
Insurer Companion Notice (Letter 2): Issued directly to the health plan's claims and appeals department, this letter demands claim reprocessing under 45 C.F.R. Part 149. It requires the carrier to recalculate cost-sharing using the Qualifying Payment Amount (QPA), issue a revised Explanation of Benefits (EOB), apply payments toward in-network deductibles, and notify the out-of-network provider to resolve payment disputes through federal Open Negotiation and IDR mechanisms rather than patient balance billing.
Collection Agency Escalation Notice (Letter 3): Deployed only if a disputed medical debt is assigned to a third-party debt collector. It invokes 15 U.S.C. § 1692g of the federal FDCPA, compelling the collection agency to cease all collection communications until written debt validation is provided. Simultaneously, it asserts state protections under 73 P.S. § 2270.4 of the Pennsylvania FCEUA, warning that failure to report the debt as "disputed by consumer" to credit reporting bureaus constitutes an automatic per se violation of the Pennsylvania UTPCPL under 73 P.S. § 201-9.2.

### Regulatory Jurisdiction Dynamics
Distinguishing between ERISA self-funded employer plans and state-regulated fully insured plans is essential when determining administrative complaint routing:
Self-Funded Group Health Plans (ERISA): Self-insured employer plans, which cover a significant portion of privately insured workers, are exempted from direct state insurance regulation under the preemption clause of 29 U.S.C. § 1144. For these plans, the PID lacks direct regulatory jurisdiction over the plan itself, making CMS and the U.S. Department of Labor the primary federal oversight bodies. However, the PID retains regulatory authority over state-licensed healthcare providers operating within Pennsylvania, enabling state regulators to sanction out-of-network physicians who issue illegal balance bills.
Fully Insured Plans: Individual plans purchased via the state exchange and fully insured commercial employer plans fall directly under PID oversight. The PID possesses statutory authority to compel claim reprocessing, enforce Act 68 prompt-pay interest penalties, and issue regulatory fines against non-compliant insurance carriers.
Interplay of Federal and State Time Limits
A comprehensive defense strategy requires managing multiple regulatory timelines simultaneously:
Internal Health Plan Appeal Window: Under ACA regulations and 29 C.F.R. § 2560.503-1, covered individuals have exactly 180 calendar days from receipt of an adverse benefit determination (EOB denial) to submit a formal internal appeal. Issuing an informal provider dispute does not toll or pause this 180-day insurance appeal clock. Consequently, the Insurer Companion Letter must be issued at Day 0 to preserve formal internal and external appeal rights.
State Public Assistance Standards: In Pennsylvania Medicaid (Medical Assistance) managed care environments under 55 Pa. Code § 1101.68, providers face a strict 180-day initial claim submission window from the service date. Insurers operating under Pennsylvania Act 68 must pay clean claims within 45 days or face a mandatory 10% annual interest penalty.
Retroactive Carrier Recoupment Limits: Under 40 Pa.C.S. § 3803, commercial health plans operating in Pennsylvania are barred from retroactively denying or recouping payments from healthcare providers past a 24-month cap from the initial payment date, except in cases involving fraud or duplicate billing.
Conclusions and Strategic Recommendations
Navigating medical debt defense in Pennsylvania requires integrating federal price protections under the No Surprises Act with state consumer protection enforcement under the FCEUA and UTPCPL.
The primary findings and recommendations include:
Concurrent Administrative Filing: Issuing simultaneous statutory dispute notices to both the healthcare provider and the health insurance carrier establishes immediate protection under 45 C.F.R. Part 149 while preserving formal 180-day internal appeal rights under ACA/ERISA frameworks.
Strict Avoidance of Partial Payments: Refraining from making partial payments on disputed medical bills prevents the inadvertent resetting of Pennsylvania’s four-year statute of limitations under 42 Pa.C.S. § 5525, preserving a complete legal defense against collection actions.
Leveraging Statutory Exposure: The Pennsylvania FCEUA explicitly binds original healthcare creditors to fair debt collection standards. Attempting to collect an unlawful balance bill subject to an active federal NSA dispute triggers per se liability under the UTPCPL (73 P.S. § 201-9.2), exposing collectors and health systems to statutory treble damages and mandatory attorney fees.
Systematic Chronological Audit: Utilizing automated tracking tools ensures strict adherence to procedural milestones, enabling administrative complaint filings with the PID (1-877-881-6388) and CMS (1-800-985-3059) precisely at Day 31.
By maintaining comprehensive records, observing statutory inflection points, and utilizing the statutory relationship between federal healthcare law and Pennsylvania debt collection statutes, consumers and advocates can resolve improper balance billing claims.
